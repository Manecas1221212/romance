# Chapter 1: Columbia, Year Two

**Fall 2003**

Anna learned how to be hungry long before Columbia.

Before the Ivy League sweatshirt and the varsity tennis bag, before the Women in Business presidency and the cocktail dresses and the boutique blowouts for recruiting mixers, there was a girl who bussed tables at a family diner and kept a jar of dollar bills under her bed. Her mother was a nurse, her stepfather repaired cars in a garage that smelled like oil and sun-baked rubber, and Anna - Anna made lists. Scholarships to apply for. Rackets to restring. Motions to memorize for debate. Ways to get out and up without losing herself in the process.

By sophomore year, she had a system.

Wake before the city. Lace up. Run Riverside, past dog walkers and joggers and the kind of men who read the Wall Street Journal as they walked. Shower. Hair in a high ponytail that said I don’t have time for nonsense. Espresso from the cart on Broadway. A banana grabbed from the sorority house kitchen. Then class—Corporate Finance at 8:40, where half the room pretended they weren’t intimidated by the problem sets.

“You’re early,” Claire said, sliding into the seat next to her and bumping shoulders like they’d done since freshman orientation.

“I like the front row.”

“You like intimidating professors into A’s.”

“Both can be true.”

Claire was the friend who always had an extra lip balm and an opinion. She was the same age, same year, with natural blonde hair cut into a practical bob and a laugh that made boys look over to see what they were missing. They were different in almost every way and matched in the only ways that mattered.

Jason slid into the row behind them, bringing the smell of expensive cologne and confidence. "Ladies," he said, as if he owned the word.

Anna turned. Jason Whitmore. Finance, like her. Legacy, unlike her. His watch cost more than her stepfather’s car, and he wore his ambition like a fitted shirt.

“Don’t forget dinner,” he murmured. “Capitol on 82nd. My treat.”

“I thought you liked women who paid for their own.” Anna smiled without looking at him.

“I like women who let me spoil them.”

Claire coughed into her notebook. “Gross.”

Anna hid her grin and opened her notebook.

Professor Jain started class with a case about hostile takeovers and stakeholder value. Anna lived for this. Numbers that turned into stories. Stories that turned into power. She took notes at a pace that made Claire’s wrist ache just watching her, asked the first question, then the second, then the one nobody expected.

After class, tennis. Dodge Fitness Center courts were always booked, but Coach made room for Anna. She was good—fluid baseline, a violent forehand when she needed it, patience when she didn’t. She had learned early that winning was mostly about choosing when to go for the line and when to keep the ball deep.

“You’re late for lives,” Coach called as Anna jogged in.

“Corporate Finance ran long.”

“Tell your corporate fiancé to wrap it up.”

“He’s not my fiancé,” Anna said too quickly.

“Mmm.” Coach threw her a ball basket. “Then stop looking at the stands and start looking at the lines.”

Anna hit until her shoulders burned and the world narrowed to rhythm. Ball, footwork, breath. By the time she finished, she checked her laptop in the locker room—her email inbox was full of messages from the Women in Business board. WiB, the club she'd joined as a freshman and somehow, by sophomore spring, found herself running. The email threads were chaotic. Food budget. Speaker honorarium. A sophomore who wanted to host a "Vision Board Night" in Low Library.

Anna typed back quickly as she headed to Hamilton Hall, laptop bag slung over her shoulder. "Pick nibbles, not a full dinner. Two interns on speaker logistics. And the vision boards are cute but we're adding a salary negotiation workshop. Nonnegotiable."

Her phone rang—her flip phone, the silver Motorola her mother had sent for emergencies. Claire's name on the tiny screen.

"Do you ever get tired?" Claire asked when Anna picked up.

"Not until the work is done," Anna said, tucking the phone against her ear.

“You sound like Jane,” Claire said when she caught up, cheeks flushed, scarf thrown around her neck five ways before class.

The name carried weight between them. Jane van Moore—three years older, already a legend by the time Anna arrived on campus. Jane had run WiB like a Fortune 500 and captained the career fair like a field marshal. When Jane spoke at the sorority house, girls actually stopped talking and listened. Now she worked downtown. Henderson & Associates. M&A. She'd done it. She'd jumped from campus to the bloodstream of the city.

Jane had taken a surprising interest in Anna. It started with an email about an internship application and ended with coffees at a place that didn’t list prices. Jane’s hair was always glossy, her blazer knife-sharp. She asked questions that felt like x-rays.

What do you actually want, Anna?

How much are you willing to trade?

“I don’t sound like Jane,” Anna said. “I sound like me.”

“You sound like you if you were wearing better shoes.”

“Rude.”

“Accurate.”

They laughed and went to lecture.

That night, the sorority house hummed like a beehive. The sisters joked it was a fraternity with better lighting, and they weren't wrong. Girls in oversized sweaters and fresh blowouts traded mascara wands and gossip. Music poured down from the third floor. Perfume hung thick in the hallway.

“Capitol, capital, whatever,” Claire sang, popping into Anna’s doorway. “Are you wearing that?”

Anna looked at herself. Black dress. Clean lines. Hair down. She wore a necklace her mother bought at a department store with a coupon and a prayer. "Is it too much?"

“It’s exactly enough.” Claire stepped in and fixed the clasp. “You look like a woman with options.”

Jason’s car smelled like leather and something woodsy. The restaurant was candlelit and pretending not to be loud. He ordered oysters. She let him. He talked internships. She talked tennis and Women in Business, and the way her mind clicked into place when a valuation finally told the truth.

“You’re different,” he said, not for the first time.

“Because I want things?”

“Because you want the right things.” He took her hand like it already belonged to him. “We’re the same, you know.”

Anna smiled and gently slid her hand back for her water glass. “Not the same. Aligned.”

On the sidewalk after, he kissed her under a streetlamp like a scene from someone else’s life. He was handsome and charming and looked good in photos. He liked that she was ambitious, as long as ambition wore heels he approved of. He liked that she was popular, as long as popularity was a halo for him too.

Anna liked him. She also liked the way the city looked over his shoulder—big, bright, busy with a thousand doors she intended to open.

"Come to Beta?" he asked, already pulling out his phone to call his friends.

“Briefly,” she said. She had a problem set due at ten.

Beta smelled like cologne and spilled beer. Boys who were going to be bankers practiced being important in rooms full of people who let them. Girls balanced plastic cups and futures on tiny heels.

"Anna!" someone shouted, as if her name were a chant. "President of WiB, breaking hearts and taking names!"

She grinned, hugged too many people, promised to mentor a freshman who didn't need mentoring so much as a calendar, and found Claire on a couch, scrolling through her Sidekick, avoiding a boy who kept staring at her.

“Tell me that you’re leaving in fifteen,” Claire said.

“Twelve.”

“Bless you.”

Jason draped an arm over Anna's shoulders in a way that read as possession to anyone paying attention. Anna noticed. She always noticed. She also let it slide—pick your battles. Tonight, she was choosing grace.

They left. Claire peeled off to the house. Anna walked Jason to his dorm and then, when the elevator doors closed, turned back toward Butler Library, the city crisp and electric around her.

She studied until midnight under chandeliers in the kind of quiet that felt like a dare. She turned the last page of her case notes and stood on the library steps, looking at the skyline, committing it to memory for no reason other than to remember 1) she was here, and 2) she had put herself here.

On the way back, her phone rang. Jane. Anna answered, voice low in the library quiet.

"Tomorrow, 7:30 a.m., Cafe Lalo," Jane said without preamble. "Wear something you could meet a CEO in."

"I have class at 9:10."

"We'll be done by 8:15. Class is not negotiable. Neither is breakfast." Jane hung up.

Anna smiled into the dark. She set an alarm for 6:20 and made another list. What to ask. What not to say. What to say when she accidentally said the thing she wasn’t going to say.

In the sorority house hallway, a freshman stopped her. "Are you Anna Bennett?"

“Sometimes.”

“I heard you got a first-round for Henderson. How?”

Anna thought of the jar of dollar bills under a bed that was now hundreds of miles away. Of her mother’s hands after a twelve-hour shift. Of the feeling of control she got when numbers aligned with reality.

“I asked,” Anna said simply. “A lot of people don’t.”

The freshman nodded like Anna had handed her a secret. Maybe she had. Secrets weren’t magic. They were mostly the courage to do the obvious thing first.

Back in her room, she crossed off the day on a paper calendar that only she used anymore. She had hit her times. She had kept her promises. She had not lost herself in any of it.

She slept like someone who had more to do tomorrow—and couldn’t wait to do it.
