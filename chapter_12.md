# Chapter 12

Tom called her into his office Friday morning.

"Close the door," he said, and Anna knew.

She sat. Composed her face. Waited.

"Anna, I got your email last night." Tom's expression was careful, clinical. "About needing a leave of absence."

"I didn't send that email."

"It came from your account."

"Someone is accessing my accounts. I've reported it multiple times, I've told you this before. IT keeps saying there's no breach, but—"

"Anna." Tom held up a hand. "I'm going to be direct. Several members of the leadership team have expressed concerns. You've seemed... scattered lately. Emails sent at odd hours with uncharacteristic errors. Meetings that appear on calendars and then don't. This leave of absence email—"

"That I didn't send."

"—suggests you're aware you're struggling."

The words hung there like a diagnosis.

"I'm not struggling," Anna said evenly. "Someone is sabotaging me."

Tom's expression didn't change, which told her everything. He thought she was cracking. Paranoid. Unable to handle the pressure.

"I'm going to suggest something," Tom said carefully. "Take a week. Go somewhere quiet. Rest. Come back clear-headed. We'll revisit your workload, maybe redistribute some accounts—"

"You're taking my accounts?"

"I'm suggesting we lighten your load."

"So you're demoting me."

"I'm protecting you!" Tom leaned forward. "Anna, you're one of the most talented people I've ever worked with. But talent doesn't matter if you burn out. Take the time. Get yourself right. Your job will be here when you come back."

Anna heard the subtext: *If you come back functional.*

She left his office with her spine straight and her hands steady. Made it to her own office. Closed the door. Sat at her desk and stared at the skyline until the shapes stopped making sense.

Her phone buzzed. Jane: *Tom told me. Don't do anything stupid.*

Anna almost laughed. Too late.

She opened her laptop and checked the logs one more time. Every suspicious email had been sent from devices registered to her—office desktop, home laptop, phone. All from her IP addresses. No evidence of hacking, spoofing, or external access.

Which meant the person doing this had physical access to her devices. Had her passwords. Knew her schedule. Knew exactly how to dismantle her life without leaving fingerprints.

Jessica.

It had to be Jessica.

Through Andrew. Through proximity. Through whatever game she was playing.

At three o'clock, Jessica appeared in Anna's doorway with tea.

"Hey," Jessica said softly. "I heard about your meeting with Tom. I'm so sorry. If there's anything I can do—"

Anna looked at her. Really looked. The concern in her eyes was a performance — convincing, but still a performance.

"Thank you," Anna said. "I'm fine."

"You don't look fine." Jessica set the tea down. "Anna, I know we work together, but I also care about you. As a person. If you need someone to talk to—"

"How's your apartment?" Anna asked suddenly.

Jessica blinked. "What?"

"Your apartment. In the West Village. Perry Street, right?"

Something flickered across Jessica's face—fear, brief and unmistakable. "How do you know where I live?"

"Public records." Anna smiled. "I'm thorough. It's what makes me good at my job."

"That's... I don't understand why you'd..." Jessica's voice wavered. She took a small step back, her hand gripping the doorframe. "Anna, I really just wanted to help. I'm not—I would never—"

"Never what?"

"I just... I should go." Jessica's eyes were wide, worried. "I have a meeting."

"Of course. Thanks for the tea."

After Jessica left, Anna poured the tea down the sink. She didn't trust anything anymore.

That evening, she went home early. The apartment felt too big, too empty. Sophie was at a sleepover. Jake was at his friend's house. Just Anna and the silence.

She opened Andrew's studio. It smelled like paint and turpentine and sadness. His latest piece was half-finished — a woman's face fractured into geometric shapes, each piece reflecting a different expression. Beautiful and broken at once.

Anna stood in front of it for a long time, trying to remember the last time Andrew had looked at her with something other than exhaustion.

She couldn't.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number: *You're losing everything. How does it feel?*

Anna called. Disconnected line, again.

She checked her email. Nothing new.

She checked the Find My Phone app. Andrew was at Perry Street. Again.

This time, Anna didn't hesitate.

She grabbed her keys, her coat, her phone. Put on sneakers instead of heels. Pulled her hair into a ponytail. Checked her reflection — she looked calm. Composed. Normal.

She drove to the West Village. Parked two blocks away. Walked to 128 Perry Street.

The building was old brick, gentrified charm, buzzer system by the door. She didn't buzz. Just waited.

Ten minutes. Twenty. Thirty.

Then the door opened. A couple leaving for dinner, laughing about something private. Anna caught the door before it closed. Slipped inside.

The lobby was small, dim. Mailboxes on one wall, stairs to the right. Anna had seen the apartment number in Jessica's employee file: 4C.

Anna climbed. Her heart was steady. Her hands weren't shaking. She felt calm in a way she hadn't in weeks—the clarity of purpose, of action instead of waiting.

Fourth floor. Four doors. 4C at the end of the hall.

Anna stood outside and listened. Voices. Andrew's laugh — the one he used to save for her. Jessica saying something Anna couldn't quite hear. More laughter.

Anna pressed her ear to the door. Strained to hear.

"—never really knew me," Andrew was saying. "She knew the version of me that was useful."

"That's so sad," Jessica murmured. "You deserve someone who sees all of you."

"Maybe. I don't know anymore."

"I see you, Andrew."

A pause. The sound of movement, fabric shifting.

Anna's hand went to the doorknob. Turned it slowly. Locked.

She stepped back. Looked at the door — standard deadbolt, no security bar visible from the gap at the bottom. Pickable, if she had tools. Breakable, if she didn't care about noise.

She stood there for another minute, listening. But either the conversation had moved or they were speaking more quietly. Or doing something that didn't require words.

Anna turned and walked back down the stairs, but instead of leaving, she waited in the lobby. Positioned herself by the mailboxes where she could see both the elevator and the stairs.

Twenty minutes passed. Thirty.

Then she heard footsteps descending. Andrew appeared, alone. His face was drawn, exhausted, but there was something softer there too — the kind of look he used to have after they made love, before all of this caos had started. Peaceful. Seen.

Anna pressed herself against the wall, out of sight, as he passed through the lobby and out the door.

She followed at a distance.

Andrew walked to the corner and raised his hand. A cab pulled over. He got in, and Anna ran to her car, pulling into traffic just in time to follow.

The cab didn't go far — just a few blocks to the waterfront, near the old piers. Andrew got out, paid, and the cab drove away.

Anna parked across the street and watched.

Andrew walked to a bench facing the Hudson. Sat down heavily, elbows on his knees, head in his hands. From this distance, in the amber glow of the streetlights, Anna could see his shoulders shaking.

He was crying.

Not the angry tears of their fights. Not the frustrated tears when his art didn't sell. This was grief — deep and unguarded, the kind you only showed when you thought no one was watching.

Anna sat in her car and watched her husband cry.

She waited there for forty minutes. Andrew didn't move. Just sat there by the river, crying over losing his sister. Or maybe crying over losing his marriage. It was impossible to tell.

Finally, he wiped his face and called another cab. Anna followed him back to what she assumed was Ben's apartment in Brooklyn. Watched him go inside.

Then she drove home through the empty streets, her mind perfectly blank.

She made herself dinner she didn't eat. Sat in the dark living room. Thought about Andrew's face in that conversation with Jessica — *I see you, Andrew* — and the way he'd cried on that bench.

She thought about her career crumbling. Her marriage shattered. The sabotage that had no fingerprints.

And somewhere in the dark hours before dawn, something shifted. Hardened. Became clear.

If this was happening, Anna needed to understand it all. Every piece. Every connection. Every betrayal.

Because you couldn't fight what you didn't understand.

And Anna had always been very, very good at understanding systems.
