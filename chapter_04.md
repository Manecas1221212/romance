# Chapter 4: Twenty years latter

Tribeca mornings flooded the loft with hard, unforgiving light. Anna liked the brightness. It kept her sharp.

She was forty-two now. Vice President of Mergers & Acquisitions at Henderson & Associates. The girl who ran on Riverside and studied under chandeliers had become the woman whose calendar decided other people's quarters. The next rung was close enough to breathe on: Senior Vice President.

"Your coffee."

Andrew slid the mug beside her laptop, careful not to touch the piles of printed models she'd brought home last night. He was barefoot, paint on his forearm, hair a little too long. He'd been a sculptor and mixed-media artist since before they met—assemblages in resin, collages of found objects. The city's art scene had never quite embraced him, but their kids adored him, and that mattered more to Anna than she usually admitted.

"You're a saint," she said, taking a sip. Perfect, as always.

Sophie appeared, ponytail perfect, stealing toast without breaking stride. "Mom, I need your signature on the field trip form."

"Put it by my bag," Anna said. "Jake?"

"Soccer," Jake yelled from the hall. "Where are my cleats?"

"By the door. Your left is under the bench," Andrew said, not looking. He always knew where things hid. It was a family miracle.

Fourteen and eight. Sophie was growing into someone sharp and self-contained. Jake was still all scraped knees and boundless energy. Anna watched them move through the morning routine—backpacks, shoelaces, the chaos that felt like normal—and felt the familiar pull between gratitude and guilt. She was good at her job. She was less certain about this one.

They kissed goodbye in the doorway, familiar and warm. Seventeen years of marriage had a texture—soft in the right places, threadbare in a few, resilient overall. Andrew squeezed her hand.

“You’ll get it,” he said.

“Get what?”

“The SVP. It’s yours.”

Anna didn’t say that something was ever “hers” until it was on letterhead. She tugged her blazer sleeve and stepped into the elevator.

Downtown was all steel and glass towers. The Henderson lobby was colder than the street outside and lit like an operating theater. Twenty years ago, Jane van Moore had sent an email to Tom Harrison that opened the door. *She's the one. Bet on her.* Anna had walked through and never looked back. Jane sat on the board now—immaculate, untouchable, the subject of constant whispered speculation about her personal life. People whispered a lot of things about powerful women.

Denise intercepted her before the turnstiles. "Morning. The board moved your SVP conversation up to next week."

Anna's heart ticked once, hard. "Next week?"

"Tuesday at three. Tom wants you prepped with the full portfolio review."

"I'll be ready."

Her office looked out on the skyline, all glass and morning light. Her name was on the door: *Anna Park, VP of Mergers & Acquisitions*. The building hummed with activity around her—deal teams moving through the halls, meetings starting, calls being made. She set down her bag and opened her laptop. Twenty years of work had led to this moment. One more rung, and she'd be exactly where she'd always planned to be.

At ten, she met with her team. Marcus walked through the pipeline; Rachel flagged a visual snag; two analysts argued about an assumption as if the model might cry. It was all routine, all productive. The work was what Anna had always been best at—clear objectives, measurable outcomes, problems that could be solved with enough analysis and determination.

The meeting ran efficiently, ending ten minutes early. Anna had trained her team well. As people filed out, she reviewed her calendar for the rest of the day. Lunch with a client, two internal reviews, a call with legal. Then home to the kids, homework, dinner. The balance she'd built over seventeen years.

At noon, Jane called. "Lunch at Mercer. Twenty minutes. Don't make me wait." She hung up before Anna could respond.

Mercer was all white tablecloths and discreet corners. Jane arrived wearing sunglasses despite the restaurant's dim lighting. She looked impeccable as always—tailored suit, hair pulled back sharply, the kind of presence that made waiters move faster.

"You have seven days to make yourself indispensable," Jane said as she sat down. "Say less in meetings. Let Tom think he's the smartest person in the room. Let the others talk themselves into corners."

"Thank you for the pep talk," Anna said. "I'll frame that advice."

Jane's mouth curved slightly. "I vouched for you twenty years ago. I'm not stopping now." She leaned back, studying Anna. "You're ready for this. You've always been ready."

They talked strategy over salads neither of them finished. Jane had been Anna's champion since the beginning—the mentor who'd opened doors, made introductions, smoothed paths. Sometimes Anna wondered what Jane saw in her, what made her worth the investment. But she'd learned not to question good fortune.

"You'll get it," Jane said when they parted. "SVP is just the beginning."

Anna returned to the office and worked through the afternoon. Emails, models, client calls. The rhythm was familiar, comfortable. This was what she was good at. This was what she'd built.

At five, she packed her bag. Her laptop went into her tote—deals didn't stop at five. She logged out of her desktop, turned off the light, and caught her reflection in the dark glass. Forty-two. VP. One step away from SVP. She looked like exactly what she'd spent two decades becoming.

The subway ride home was crowded, anonymous. Anna stood with her hand on the pole, her tote heavy with work, and watched the tunnel lights blur past. She thought about the promotion, about what SVP would mean—more influence, more visibility, closer to the board. Everything she'd planned for since Columbia.

At home, Andrew was cooking something that smelled like garlic and tomatoes. Jake was sprawled on the floor doing homework with one eye on his tablet. Sophie was on the couch with headphones in, probably texting friends.

"Mom's home!" Jake yelled without looking up.

"How was it?" Andrew asked, stirring something on the stove.

"Good. Long. The SVP meeting got moved up to next week."

He turned, wooden spoon in hand. "That's great, right?"

"I think so." She set down her bag and kissed his cheek. He smelled like paint and pasta sauce. "What are we having?"

"Puttanesca. Sophie says it's 'too salty' but she always eats three servings."

Anna smiled. This was her life. The work she'd built, the family she'd chosen, the balance she'd maintained. Twenty years after leaving Jason and Columbia behind, she'd gotten exactly what she'd wanted.

She changed out of her work clothes and joined them for dinner. Sophie complained about chemistry. Jake talked about soccer tryouts. Andrew told a story about a gallery owner who wanted to commission a piece. Normal. Good. Real.

Later, after the kids were in bed, Anna opened her laptop at the kitchen table. Andrew was in his studio—she could hear music playing faintly through the wall. She reviewed the portfolio materials for her SVP conversation, making notes, refining arguments, preparing.

She'd worked too hard to leave anything to chance.

By midnight, she was satisfied. She closed the laptop and looked around the loft—the exposed brick, the high ceilings, the life she'd built brick by brick, choice by choice. She'd made it. And next week, she'd take the next step.

Anna went to bed beside Andrew, who was already asleep, and didn't dream of anything at all.
