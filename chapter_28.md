# Chapter 28: The Board

Three months later, Anna got the call.
The phone was warm against her palm, the late-morning Manhattan light bleaching her desk a surgical white. She felt the familiar lift of adrenaline and the quieter undertow beneath it—calm, practiced, almost like breath held.

"Congratulations, Anna. The board voted unanimously. You're in."

She was the youngest board member in Henderson & Associates' seventy-year history. Forty-three years old. Power she'd dreamed of since Columbia.

The press release went out. *Anna Park Appointed to Henderson & Associates Board. First Woman Under 50 to Achieve Role.*
Her inbox bloomed with congratulations and calendar pings; somewhere, a PR assistant queued posts that translated ambition into the neutral language of achievement.

The articles called her a trailblazer. A survivor. An inspiration.

If they only knew. The sentence sat in her chest like a coin—weighty, cold, never spent.

The first board meeting was surreal. Anna sat in Jane's old seat. Same chair. Same view of the table.
The room smelled faintly of lemon oil and expensive leather. The reclaimed walnut table held hairline scratches like ghosted signatures.

She could almost feel Jane's presence. Watching. Approving. Measuring. A trace of memory—Jane's perfume, cool and precise—seemed to hang in the conditioned air.

"Welcome, Anna," said David Chen, board chair. "We're thrilled to have you." His cufflinks flashed with the screen's reflected graphs; his voice carried the smooth edge of a man who had learned to make demands sound like favors.

The meeting covered quarterly numbers. Strategic planning. M&A pipeline. Anna contributed. Showed she belonged. Even as she spoke, she tracked the room—Patricia's measured nods, Gerald's conservative stillness, the way silence pooled before decisions.

After, David pulled her aside.

"Anna, I want you know I'm very aware of Jane's... situation."

"Of course."

"The board wants to ensure we've moved past that chapter. That Henderson's reputation is intact."

"I understand."

"Good. Because we're counting on you to represent a new generation of leadership. Ethical. Transparent. Clean."
The words clicked into place like a compliance training module. Anna watched them pass over the table and vanish into the carpet.

Anna smiled. "I won't let you down."

She meant it. She'd learned from Jane's mistakes. The biggest one: getting caught. Never write what can be spoken. Never speak what can be implied. Keep compartments airtight.

---

Andrew remarried a year later. Catherine, the gallery owner. A small ceremony. Sophie and Jake as witnesses. Anna pictured a narrow room washed with afternoon sun, white flowers with clean stems, vows spoken in voices that didn't tremble.

Anna sent a gift. A painting—expensive, tasteful. No message needed. She chose it clinically, by palette and provenance, the way one selects a hedge rather than a sentiment.

Catherine sent a thank-you note. Handwritten. Gracious. The loops of her script were open and unguarded. Anna noted the openness the way a surgeon notes a healthy rhythm.

Anna filed it away and felt nothing. The quiet inside her had become reliable, like a room where the windows didn't open.

Sophie was fifteen now. Starting to think about colleges. Wanted to study medicine. As far from her mother's world as possible.

"Do you hate me?" Anna asked once, over a lunch Sophie had agreed to out of obligation. The bistro was narrow, all white tile and soft brass; lemon in the water, a napkin folded with military precision. Sophie kept her phone face down and her shoulders squared.

Sophie considered the question seriously. "No. I'm sad for you."

"Why?"

"Because you have everything you wanted and you're still empty."

The insight was sharper than any accusation. It slid between ribs without resistance.

---

Jake was nine. Looked like Andrew. Had Andrew's quiet kindness. He visited Anna every other weekend because the custody agreement said he had to. His hands, when he lifted the water glass, were Andrew's—careful, unhurried.

They'd started running out of things to talk about. The room's curated music covered the pauses like a polite blanket.

Now they sat in expensive restaurants and made small talk about school, about friends, about nothing that mattered.

"Do you ever think about her?" Jake asked one Sunday. The question arrived as gently as he did everything, and still it ruptured the surface.

"Who?"

"The woman who died. Jessica."

Anna's blood went cold. "Sometimes. Why?" She set her fork down to keep from gripping it.

"Dad said she was nice. That she made him happy for a while. Before."

Before she died. Before Anna killed her.

"I'm sorry she's gone," Anna said carefully.

"Me too. Dad cries about her sometimes. When he thinks no one's listening."

Anna absorbed that. Andrew still grieved Jessica. Still mourned the woman Anna had murdered.

"Your father loved her?"

"I think so. Not like he loved you. But yeah."

Anna paid the check. Drove Jake back to Andrew's house. Watched him run inside without looking back. She sat in the car and thought about how many lives she'd destroyed in service of her ambition. The dashboard clock ticked forward with the indifference of an instrument that only knows measure, not meaning.

Jessica. Dead.

Andrew. Remarried—apparently steady; inside, still broken.

Sophie and Jake. Damaged.

Jane. Imprisoned.

Claire. Distant.

And Anna? Sitting on a board. Commanding a salary. Owning power.

Hollow. Empty. Alone.

She drove back to her penthouse. Poured expensive wine. Stood at the window overlooking the city. The glass was double-paned; the city reached her as vibration more than sound. The wine tasted mineral and metallic on her tongue.

All those lights. All those lives. All those people who'd never know her name, her power, her sins.

Her phone buzzed. A message from an unknown number. The tone—a default chime she'd never bothered to change—sounded oddly like a door unlocking.

*Congratulations on the board. I always knew you'd make it. —J*

Jane. From prison. Somehow still reaching her.

Anna didn't reply. But she didn't delete it either. The message sat there like a small, deliberate stain.

Because part of her—the darkest part—wanted Jane to know she'd won.

Wanted Jane to see what her mentorship had created.

A woman who'd sacrificed everything human to become powerful. Power felt like the quiet in an operating room—controlled, bright, and smelling faintly of antiseptic.

A woman who looked in the mirror and saw a stranger.

A woman who'd gotten exactly what she wanted and lost everything that mattered.

Anna finished the wine. Went to bed. The apartment exhaled around her—systems whirring, temperatures held, nothing out of place.

And dreamed, as always, of Jessica's face.

Not guilt—something colder. In the dream, West Village light banded her face; her expression withheld judgment. Only the knowledge that some costs, once paid, are nonrefundable.
